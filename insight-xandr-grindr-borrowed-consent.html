Norway fines Xandr €8.6m — relying on a partner's consent

Vishal Akshintala · 2026-11-24

The app collected the consent. The ad platform paid the fine.

Norway's data protection authority has fined Xandr Inc., now part of Microsoft, €8.6m (final decision of 2 October 2026, published by the EDPB on 16 October). The case started in 2020 with a complaint from the Norwegian Consumer Council.

During real-time bidding inside Grindr, Xandr processed at least the fact that a person used the app, their advertising ID and their IP address. Because Grindr is aimed at gay, bi, trans and queer people, the authority treated that as data concerning sexual orientation, a special category under the GDPR, which needed explicit consent.

Xandr relied on the consent Grindr collected. The authority found that consent was not valid, and held Xandr responsible as a joint controller: each controller has to check that the consent is valid and covers its own processing.

€8.6m fine on Xandr Inc., now part of Microsoft.

  • Started with: a 2020 complaint by the Norwegian Consumer Council
  • App: Grindr, a location-based dating app
  • Breaches: GDPR Articles 6(1) and 9(1)

Using the app said something

  • In real-time bidding, the split-second auction for an ad slot, Xandr processed at least the fact that a person used Grindr, their advertising ID and IP address.
  • Grindr is aimed at gay, bi, trans and queer people, so the authority treated this as data concerning sexual orientation (Art. 9(1), GDPR).

  • The assumption the decision rejects:
  • Each controller checks the consent covers its own processing.
  • My summary of the authority's reasoning, as published by the EDPB

What the authority found:

  • Xandr was a joint controller, as both supply-side and demand-side platform;
  • explicit consent was the relevant legal basis for this data;
  • Xandr relied on Grindr's consent, which was not valid under the GDPR;
  • the infringements were at least negligent and of high seriousness.
  • EDPB summary of the decision, 16 Oct 2026

Four questions to ask

  • Could this data reveal health, sexuality or another special category?
  • Does the consent cover your processing, not just the partner's?
  • Have you checked how the consent was collected, not only that it exists?
  • Could you show that check to a regulator?
