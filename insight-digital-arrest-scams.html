Digital Arrest Scams and the Law's Slow Response to a Fast-Moving Fraud

Vishal Akshintala

"Digital arrest" is not a legal process. No Indian law enforcement agency conducts an arrest, or holds anyone in custody, over a video call. And yet the scam built on precisely that false premise has become one of the most financially destructive fraud patterns operating in India today — a scammer impersonating police, customs, or a central investigating agency, staging a fabricated video-call "arrest," and coercing a victim into transferring funds under sustained psychological pressure, often over hours.

The scam works not because the legal claim is sophisticated, but because it exploits an asymmetry: most people do not know precisely what a lawful arrest, summons, or investigation actually requires procedurally, and that uncertainty is the entire attack surface. A scammer does not need a victim to believe a false legal theory in detail — only to be frightened enough not to pause and ask a lawyer, a family member, or even a search engine whether any of this is how Indian criminal procedure actually functions.

The correct legal response to a digital arrest call is the same in every case, regardless of which agency is impersonated: no lawful process requires payment over a video call, and no genuine investigating officer conducts one that way.

From a legal-response standpoint, the relevant framework is the IT Act's provisions on cheating by personation (Section 66D) and the underlying fraud provisions of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, combined with the procedural machinery built for exactly this kind of financial-fraud urgency: the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal's account-freeze mechanism and the 1930 helpline, both of which exist specifically because speed determines whether transferred funds can still be recovered before they're moved through further accounts.

What the law has been slower to build is the preventive side: a standardized, well-publicized statement of exactly what a lawful arrest and investigation procedure does and does not involve, distributed with the same reach as the scam itself. Financial institutions have started adding in-app warnings at the point of a high-value transfer; that pattern deserves to be standardized rather than left to individual banks' discretion.

For anyone who has received such a call: end it immediately, do not transfer funds under any circumstance regardless of claimed urgency, and report at cybercrime.gov.in or via 1930 without delay — the freeze mechanism only works within a narrow window before funds move further.
